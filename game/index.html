What even is… Minecraft?

Minecraft is a game about placing and breaking blocks. On the surface, that sounds simple. In practice, it became one of the best-selling video games ever made.

It started in 2009, when Markus Persson — known online as Notch — built a prototype inspired by Infiniminer, a small indie game that let players dig through randomly generated terrain. Notch took that core idea and opened it up: build anything, anywhere, in a world that goes on as far as the engine can count.

An early public release in 2009 picked up a following almost immediately. The game grew fast — too fast, at times. Servers crashed under the load. Notch made it briefly free to compensate. People built impossible things: working computers, scale replicas of the Starship Enterprise, entire cities. The game had barely launched and it was already a phenomenon.

Minecraft officially released in November 2011 at MineCon, by which point millions of people had already been playing it for years. Shortly after, Jens Bergensten — Jeb — took over creative direction as Notch moved on to other projects. Mojang kept updating: new biomes, new mobs, new mechanics. Tameable wolves, the Nether, the End, pistons, redstone circuits, horses, ocean monuments, a whole archaeology system — the game kept accreting layers.

Microsoft acquired Mojang in 2014 for $2.5 billion. Development continued. By the 2020s, Minecraft had crossed 300 million copies sold across all platforms — Java, Bedrock, mobile, console — making it the single best-selling video game in history. The monthly active player count sits in the hundreds of millions.

It's a building game, a survival game, a canvas, a programming environment, a classroom tool, and — for a lot of people — one of the first games they ever played seriously. The version you remember might be very different from the version someone else holds in their head. That's kind of the point.
